## What is Informatics?

Informatics sits at the intersection of three domains:

| Domain | Focus |
|--------|-------|
| **Data** | Raw facts, figures, observations |
| **Systems** | Technologies that store, process, transmit information |
| **Society** | People who create, use, and are affected by information systems |

Informatics involves examining how information technologies are developed, applied, and utilized in society.

That one-sentence definition is doing a lot of work, so let's unpack it. First, informatics is about *examining*: it is a field of study, a way of asking questions, not just a set of technical skills. Second, it cares about the entire life of a technology: how it gets *developed* (who builds it, and with what assumptions?), how it gets *applied* (to which problems, in which industries?), and how it gets *utilized* (what do people actually do with it once it's in their hands, which is often very different from what its designers intended?). Third, and most importantly, all of this happens *in society*. You cannot fully understand a hospital records system, a search engine, or a chatbot by studying only its code. You also have to study the people who use it, the organizations that deploy it, and the communities it affects.

This is why the three-circle picture above matters. A spreadsheet full of numbers (data) is inert until software (systems) organizes it and a person (society) interprets it and acts on it. Remove any one circle and you no longer have informatics; you have statistics, or engineering, or sociology. Informatics lives in the overlap, and throughout this course we will keep returning to that overlap.

:::{note} Informatics in the Age of AI
When this field was named, "information technology" mostly meant databases, networks, and websites. Today, artificial intelligence is woven through every informatics subdomain: health informatics uses AI to read medical images, legal informatics uses it to search case law, and you probably used it to answer a question this week. Throughout this course, we'll keep asking the same three questions about AI that we ask about every technology: What data does it use? How does the system work? And who in society benefits, or is harmed?
:::

### Informatics and Its Neighbors

Informatics has several sibling fields, and people (including universities) use the labels inconsistently. A rough map helps:

| Field | Central question | Typical emphasis |
|-------|-----------------|------------------|
| **Computer science** | How can computation be done, and done efficiently? | Algorithms, programming languages, theory of computation |
| **Information science** | How do people create, record, seek, and use information? | Documents, meaning, organization of knowledge, libraries and archives |
| **Data science** | What patterns and predictions can we extract from data? | Statistics, machine learning, analytics |
| **Informatics** | How do information technologies work within human and social contexts? | The interaction of data, systems, and society in specific domains |

Computer science tends to focus on the machines and the mathematics behind them. Information science, the older sibling that grew out of librarianship and documentation, focuses on recorded information and the humans who need it. Data science, the newest arrival, focuses on squeezing insight out of large datasets. Informatics borrows from all three but keeps its eye on application and context: an informaticist is usually asking how information technology changes the practice of medicine, or law, or education, or music, and how those domains push back and change the technology.

Interestingly, the naming difference is partly geographic. What English speakers call "computer science," much of Europe has long called *informatique* (French), *informatica* (Italian and Dutch), or *Informatik* (German). As Gleick points out in this week's reading, the word itself signals that computing has always really been about information.

### A Field of Many Fields: Informatics Subdomains

Because informatics is about technology *in context*, there is an informatics of almost every context. Whatever your major, there is probably an informatics subdomain waiting for you. A sampling:

| Subdomain | One-line description |
|-----------|---------------------|
| **Health informatics** | Manages clinical information: electronic health records, hospital systems, patient data. Includes specialties like nursing, dental, and pharmaceutical informatics |
| **Bioinformatics** | Applies computing to biological data, especially DNA sequences and protein structures |
| **Cheminformatics** | Uses computational methods to store, search, and analyze chemical compounds, often for drug discovery |
| **Agricultural informatics** | Brings sensors, data, and modeling to farming: soil monitoring, yield prediction, precision agriculture |
| **Business informatics** | Designs and studies the information systems that run organizations, from inventory to customer analytics |
| **Legal informatics** | Applies technology to law: searchable case databases, e-discovery, contract analysis |
| **Educational informatics** | Studies learning technologies, learning analytics, and systems like Canvas |
| **Environmental informatics** | Manages environmental data: climate models, pollution sensors, ecosystem monitoring |
| **Geoinformatics** | Works with geographic and spatial data: GIS, mapping, GPS-based services |
| **Music informatics** | Analyzes and retrieves music computationally: recommendation systems, automatic transcription |
| **Cultural informatics** | Digitizes and organizes cultural heritage: museum collections, digital archives |
| **Social informatics** | Studies how information technologies and social life shape each other |
| **Sports informatics** | Applies data and sensing to athletics: performance analytics, wearables, strategy |
| **Military informatics** | Applies information systems to defense: logistics, communications, intelligence |
| **AI and computational informatics** | Builds and studies intelligent systems and the computational methods underneath the other subdomains |

Two things to take away from this list. First, informatics is a team sport between disciplines: a bioinformatician needs biology *and* computing, a legal informaticist needs law *and* information systems. Second, this list is a career map. In class we'll discuss which of these areas overlap with your own major and interests, because for most students the answer is "more of them than you'd expect."

### The Data-Information-Knowledge-Wisdom Pyramid

Understanding the transformation from raw data to wisdom:

- **Data**: Discrete, objective facts about an event (Who? What? Where? When? How many?)
- **Information**: Data with analyzed relationships and connections
- **Knowledge**: Contextualized information (How?)
- **Wisdom**: Applied, reflected upon, embedded with values (Why do?)

This transformation is what enables real-world decision-making!

To see the pyramid in action, imagine your fitness tracker. The raw sensor readings (heart rate 142, 9:03 p.m., 4,200 steps) are **data**: discrete facts with no story attached. When the app relates those facts to each other ("your resting heart rate has risen 8% over two weeks while your sleep has dropped below six hours"), you have **information**: data organized so that relationships become visible. When you combine that information with context and experience ("this pattern usually shows up during finals week; last semester it preceded getting sick"), you have **knowledge**: an understanding of how things work. And when you act with judgment and values ("my health matters more than one more hour of cramming, so I'm going to bed"), you're exercising **wisdom**: knowing not just how, but why, and what *ought* to be done.

Each layer of the pyramid requires the one below it, and each involves more human involvement than the last. Machines are excellent at collecting data and increasingly good at producing information. Knowledge requires context that often lives in people's heads. Wisdom involves values, ethics, and judgment, which is exactly why this course pairs technical topics with social ones.

One caution: the pyramid is a useful teaching model, not a law of nature. Real information work is messier. Data are never perfectly "objective" (someone decided what to measure and how), and knowledge sometimes shapes what we accept as data in the first place. Treat the pyramid as a map, and remember that maps simplify.

## How Do We Define Information?

"Information" might be the most-used and least-examined word in this whole field. This week's readings offer two classic attempts to pin it down, and they approach the problem from opposite directions.

### Buckland's Three Senses of Information

Michael Buckland, in "Information as Thing" (Buckland, 1991), points out that when people say "information" they usually mean one of three different things:

1. **Information-as-process**: the act of informing, of becoming informed. When a friend tells you your class is canceled, the *telling* (and your change in knowledge) is information in this sense. It is an event that happens to a person.
2. **Information-as-knowledge**: the intangible stuff that gets communicated. What you now know (class is canceled) is information in this sense. It lives in minds, and you cannot touch it or measure it directly.
3. **Information-as-thing**: the physical objects that carry or represent information: documents, books, files, databases, photographs, even museum specimens. The text message on your screen is information in this sense.

Buckland's provocative argument is that the third sense deserves serious attention, because *things* are the only form of information that systems can actually store, retrieve, and process. A library cannot shelve knowledge; it shelves books. A database cannot hold your understanding; it holds records. Whenever we build information systems, we are inevitably working with information-as-thing, and hoping that the things we deliver will spark the process that produces knowledge. Keep this three-way distinction in your pocket; it will clarify many muddled conversations about technology, in this course and beyond.

### Shannon's Communication Model

Where Buckland asks *what information is*, Claude Shannon asked *how it moves*. In his landmark 1948 paper "A Mathematical Theory of Communication," Shannon (1948) wrote:

> "The fundamental problem of communication is that of reproducing at one point either exactly or approximately a message selected at another point."

Shannon's model breaks every act of communication into a chain of parts:

| Component | Role | Example (a text message) |
|-----------|------|--------------------------|
| **Sender** | Originates the message | You, deciding what to say |
| **Encoder** | Converts the message into a transmittable signal | Your phone turning text into radio waves |
| **Channel** | The medium the signal travels through | The cellular network |
| **Noise** | Anything that distorts the signal | Interference, a weak connection, a typo |
| **Decoder** | Converts the signal back into a message | Your friend's phone rendering the text |
| **Receiver** | The destination of the message | Your friend, reading it |

Later communication scholars added **feedback** (the receiver's response flowing back to the sender) to describe conversations rather than one-way transmissions. The model's genius is its generality: it describes talking across a noisy room, a fiber-optic cable under the ocean, and a DNA strand copying itself. Shannon deliberately set *meaning* aside ("frequently the messages have meaning," he noted dryly) so that information could be measured and engineered. That move made the digital world possible, and it is also why informatics needs Buckland too: engineering can move messages, but only people make meaning.

Separate from Shannon's transmission model, information professionals also describe an **information lifecycle**: information is *created*, *disseminated*, *stored, indexed, and retrieved*, *consumed*, and eventually *archived or disposed of*. This lifecycle model comes from the information management tradition, not from Shannon; it tracks what happens to documents and data over time, while Shannon's model captures a single act of transmission. You'll use both lenses in this course: the lifecycle when we discuss databases, archives, and retention; the communication chain when we discuss networks, noise, and encoding.

## Key Concepts

### What is Information Science?

According to Bawden & Robinson (2012):

> "Information science can best be understood as a field of study, with human recorded information as its concern, focusing on the components of the communication chain, studied through the perspective of domain analysis."

Information science is concerned with:
1. Computing, algorithms, and information technologies
2. Entropy in information theory and physics
3. Information recorded in documents, with meaning and knowledge

Note the phrase "communication chain" in that definition: it is Shannon's model again, extended to cover everything that happens to recorded information between an author and a reader. And "domain analysis" means studying information within specific communities and fields, which is exactly the move that produces the informatics subdomains you saw above.

What kinds of questions does such a field ask? Information scientists have proposed some big ones. Marcia Bates frames the field around three: What are the features and laws of the recorded-information universe? How do people relate to, seek, and use information? And how can access to recorded information be made most rapid and effective? Andrew Dillon pushes further, asking what the essential nature of information is such that the same word applies to communicating, sustaining biological life, and learning; how we move beyond merely *providing* information (storage, retrieval, management) toward shaping how information nourishes a culture, an organization, or a person; and how we can positively influence the world's cyberinfrastructure as the rest of humanity comes online. Keep these questions in mind: nearly every week of this course is a partial answer to one of them.

### Gleick on Information

> "Every new medium transforms the nature of human thought. In the long run, history is the story of information becoming aware of itself."

> "We can see now that information is what our world runs on: the blood and the fuel, the vital principle. It pervades the sciences from top to bottom, transforming every branch of knowledge."

Gleick's *The Information* (2011) tells the long history behind those claims, and the excerpt you're reading gives you its flavor. He opens far from Silicon Valley, with the talking drums of West Africa: drummers could relay complex messages across miles by mimicking the tones of spoken language, a working long-distance information network centuries before the telegraph. European colonizers marveled at the speed of the drums without understanding the encoding, a nice reminder that every information technology depends on shared codes. From there Gleick traces the alphabet (a technology so successful we forget it *is* one: a small set of symbols that can encode any utterance), the dictionary, the telegraph, and finally the **bit**.

The bit, short for *binary digit*, is Shannon's unit for measuring information: a single yes-or-no, on-or-off, one-or-zero. As Gleick puts it, the bit is "a fundamental particle of a different sort: not just tiny but abstract," and some scientists now suspect it may be more fundamental than matter itself, with information forming "the very core of existence." Whether or not you buy that grand claim, the practical point stands: once information could be *measured*, it could be compressed, transmitted, priced, and engineered. Everything digital you own runs on that idea.

:::{tip} From Shannon to ChatGPT
Claude Shannon, the founder of information theory, ran an experiment in the 1940s: he asked people to guess the next letter of a partially hidden sentence, showing that human language is highly *predictable*. Today's AI chatbots are, at their core, that same idea made industrial: enormously powerful systems for predicting the next word. The 75-year-old theory you're reading about this week is the foundation of the newest technology in your pocket. We'll return to this idea in Modules 12 and 13.
:::

### Nye: Can We Define Technology?

If "information" is slippery, "technology" is worse. David Nye's chapter asks what the word even means, and his answers will change how you look at every device you own.

Start with the word itself. The root is the Greek *techne*, meaning "art, skill, craft, or technique," combined with the suffix *-ology*, "a branch of learning." When "technology" was coined in English in the 17th century, it meant a treatise on the mechanic arts: a *book about* skills, not the machines themselves. Only in the 1930s did social scientists stretch the word to describe the sweeping transformations of the industrial and corporate world, and Nye notes that in the second half of the twentieth century it became "an annoyingly vague abstraction." The word we use constantly became part of everyday English barely a century ago.

Nye's deeper argument is that tools are inseparable from human society. Tools are older than written language, and perhaps (as chimpanzees' termite-fishing sticks suggest) older than spoken language. They are never merely passive objects. "Technologies are not just objects but also the skills needed to use them," Nye writes; daily life is saturated with **tacit knowledge** of tools and machines, the know-how you carry without being able to fully write it down (try writing complete instructions for riding a bicycle). Every society both invents tools and borrows them from other cultures, assembling its own technological repertoire, and those tools are woven into music, shelter, food, and meaning.

Nye also makes a striking claim about *why* we have technology at all: not primarily for necessities. Humans secured food and shelter very early. Instead, "technologies have been used for social evolution." And the relationship between tools and problems runs backwards from what you'd expect: "The tool often exists before the problem to be solved. Latent in every tool are unforeseen transformations." The laser was invented before most of its uses; so was the internet.

Finally, Nye gives us a vocabulary for one of this course's recurring debates. **Technological determinism** is the idea that technology develops along its own inevitable path and drives social change, with humans along for the ride. Nye's emblem for this view is a single cut in Stanley Kubrick's film *2001: A Space Odyssey*: a prehistoric ancestor of modern humans picks up a bone, uses it as a weapon, and hurls it into the air, where it spins and match-cuts into an orbiting spacecraft, as if "a direct line of inevitable technological development led from the first tools to the conquest of the stars." The opposing view, often called the **social construction of technology**, holds that societies choose, shape, and redirect their tools; nothing about the bone made the space station inevitable. Nye sides with choice over inevitability, and as you meet technologies in this course (search engines, autonomous cars, AI), you should keep asking: is this happening *to* us, or are people making decisions we could contest?

### From Ideas to Instructions

One more idea from this week's lectures ties the human world to the machine world. When you use a computer, you almost never address the machine directly; you work through an **application**, which translates human ideas into operations a computer can perform. Underneath every application are **procedures**: step-by-step instructions for completing a task. Computers execute procedures with perfect literalness. They will not notice that you forgot a step, reversed an order, or meant something other than what you wrote. That is why **programming languages** exist: human speech is imprecise, so we need languages that both humans and machines can interpret exactly. We'll revisit this idea when we discuss software, databases, and algorithms later in the course; for now, remember that every app on your phone is a bundle of frozen procedures, written by people, embodying their choices.
