## Copyright Basics

### What Copyright Protects

Copyright automatically protects original creative works:
- Literary works (books, articles, code)
- Musical compositions and recordings
- Visual art (photographs, paintings)
- Movies and videos
- Architectural works

"Automatically" is the key word: the moment you fix an original work in tangible form (write the essay, snap the photo, record the riff), copyright exists. No registration, no fee, no © symbol required. You have been accumulating copyrights your whole life.

### What Copyright DOESN'T Protect

- Ideas (only the expression of ideas)
- Facts
- Titles, names, short phrases
- Works in the public domain
- **Works generated entirely by AI** (more on this in [The Law in Motion](ai-and-net-neutrality.md)!)

The idea/expression line deserves a beat: copyright protects *how you said it*, never *what you said*. Anyone may write a novel about a boy wizard at a magic school; no one may copy J.K. Rowling's sentences or her particular characters. Facts are free to all, which is why journalists can report each other's scoops and databases of raw facts get thin protection.

### The Public Domain

The **public domain** is the pool of creative work that belongs to everyone, free to copy, adapt, remix, and sell. Works arrive there by several routes: copyright *expires* (in the US, works published before a rolling cutoff enter the public domain each January 1, a date fans of old cartoons now celebrate as Public Domain Day; the earliest Mickey Mouse cartoon, *Steamboat Willie*, entered in 2024); the work was *never eligible* (facts, US federal government works, and, as you'll see in [The Law in Motion](ai-and-net-neutrality.md), purely AI-generated output); or the creator *dedicates* it voluntarily. Every Disney fairy-tale blockbuster is built on public-domain source material, which gives the company's long history of lobbying to extend copyright terms a certain irony worth discussing.

```{figure} images/public-domain-paths.svg
:alt: Diagram of the three routes into the public domain. Route one, copyright expires: protection lasts the author's life plus seventy years, then the work enters the pool each January first, Public Domain Day, as Steamboat Willie did in 2024. Route two, never eligible: facts, US federal government works, and purely AI-generated output start in the public domain. Route three, voluntary dedication: the creator gives the work to everyone. All three arrows flow into one pool labeled the public domain, free to copy, adapt, remix, and sell.
:width: 100%

Three roads, one destination: whether a work outlives its term, was never protectable, or was given away, once it lands in the pool it belongs to everyone, forever.
```

## Fair Use

**Fair Use** allows limited use of copyrighted material without permission for purposes like:

- **Criticism and Commentary**
- **News Reporting**
- **Teaching and Education**
- **Research**
- **Parody**

### The Four Factors

Courts consider:
1. **Purpose**: Is it commercial or educational? Is the use *transformative*?
2. **Nature**: Is the original work creative or factual?
3. **Amount**: How much did you use?
4. **Effect**: Does it harm the market for the original?

```{figure} images/fair-use-factors.svg
:alt: Diagram of the four fair-use factors as four cards feeding a judge's balance. Factor one, purpose and character: educational, transformative, or parody leans fair; commercial repackaging leans unfair. Factor two, nature of the original: factual or published leans fair; creative or unpublished leans unfair. Factor three, amount and substantiality: a small excerpt leans fair; whole chapters or the heart of the work lean unfair. Factor four, market effect: no lost sales leans fair; substituting for the original leans unfair. Arrows from all four cards point to a balance scale labeled weighed together, case by case, not a checklist.
:width: 100%
:name: fig-fair-use-factors

No single factor decides a fair-use case: each one is a sliding scale, and the court weighs all four together, which is why even expert lawyers guess wrong.
```

Each factor is a sliding scale, not a checkbox, and courts weigh all four together. A mini-example for each:

1. **Purpose and character.** A film critic quoting dialogue to review a movie leans fair (commentary, noncommercial-ish, adds new meaning). A t-shirt company printing the same dialogue to sell shirts leans unfair (commercial, adds nothing). Parody gets special protection: 2 Live Crew's raunchy remake of "Oh, Pretty Woman" was fair use precisely because parody *must* borrow from its target.
2. **Nature of the original.** Quoting a factual news report leans fair; copying from an unpublished novel leans unfair, because creative and unpublished works get stronger protection than factual ones. Facts want to circulate; art gets more shelter.
3. **Amount and substantiality.** Quoting two sentences of a book leans fair; posting a chapter does not. But quality matters as much as quantity: a magazine that quoted only about 300 words from President Ford's memoir still lost, because those words (the Nixon pardon explanation) were "the heart of the work."
4. **Market effect.** Recording a TV show to watch later was fair use (no market harm, said the Supreme Court in the Betamax case); posting the same show to YouTube substitutes for the original and harms its market. Ask: does the use compete with, or replace, the original or its licensing market?

### Transformative Use, and What *Warhol* Changed

Running through factor one is the idea of **transformative use**: does the new work add new expression, meaning, or purpose rather than merely repackaging the original? For years, many assumed that making something look or feel different was enough. The Supreme Court corrected that assumption in *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith* (2023). Photographer Lynn Goldsmith had taken a portrait of Prince; Warhol silkscreened it into his instantly recognizable pop-art style; decades later the Warhol Foundation licensed the resulting *Orange Prince* image to a magazine for a Prince cover story. The Court held, 7 to 2, that the first factor turns on the purpose of the *use*, not on how visually different the new work is. Licensing an image of Prince to illustrate a magazine article about Prince was exactly the commercial purpose Goldsmith's photo served, so factor one favored Goldsmith. Warhol's artistic transformation did not settle the question; the competing *use* did. The practical lesson for you: "I changed it a lot" is not a fair-use argument by itself. Ask instead whether your use serves a different purpose from the original or substitutes for it in the same market.

:::{warning} Fair Use is Not Clear-Cut
Fair use is determined case-by-case. When in doubt, get permission or use openly licensed content. And note what fair use is *not*: it is a defense you argue after being sued, not a license you hold in advance. Even musicians with expert lawyers guess wrong in both directions; Ed Sheeran spent years in court before a jury found in 2023 that "Thinking Out Loud" did not infringe Marvin Gaye's "Let's Get It On."
:::

## Open Licensing Alternatives

Not all content is traditionally copyrighted:

| License Type | What It Means |
|--------------|---------------|
| **Public Domain** | No copyright restrictions; free to use |
| **Creative Commons** | Author-chosen permissions (attribution, non-commercial, etc.) |
| **Open Source** | Software with freely available source code |
| **Copyleft** | Derivative works must use the same license |

```{figure} images/rights-spectrum.svg
:alt: Spectrum diagram of usage rights running from most open on the left to most restricted on the right. Public domain: no rights reserved, free to copy, adapt, remix, and sell. Creative Commons: some rights reserved, with author-chosen conditions such as attribution or non-commercial use. Open source and copyleft: licensed with conditions, source freely available, and copyleft requires derivatives to carry the same license. All rights reserved: full copyright, the automatic default, permission needed for most uses. An arrow beneath the four cards shows fewer restrictions on the left and more restrictions on the right.
:width: 100%
:name: fig-rights-spectrum

Copyright's default is "all rights reserved," so everything to the left of it exists only because a creator (or the law) deliberately gave rights away.
```
