## What is Intellectual Property?

**Intellectual Property (IP)** refers to creations of the mind that are legally protected.

### Types of IP Protection

| Type | Protects | Duration | Example |
|------|----------|----------|---------|
| **Copyright** | Creative works | Life + 70 years | Books, music, movies |
| **Patents** | Inventions | 20 years (from filing) | New technology, processes |
| **Trademarks** | Brand identifiers | Indefinite (if renewed) | Logos, brand names |
| **Trade Secrets** | Confidential business info | As long as kept secret | Formulas, processes |

### IP in Your Daily Life

The table looks abstract until you realize you brush against all four types before lunch:

- **Copyright** governs the meme you reposted this morning (someone owns that screenshot or photo), the song sample in a track you streamed (recording artists license samples or get sued; the hip-hop sampling wars of the 1990s built a whole licensing industry), the fan fiction you read (technically a derivative work of the original author's characters, usually tolerated but rarely *licensed*), and the code you copy from a tutorial (open-source licenses are copyright licenses).
- **Trademarks** are why the swoosh on your shoes, the golden arches you passed, and even a specific shade of robin's-egg blue on a jewelry box are legally protected. A trademark does not protect the *thing*; it protects consumers from confusion about *who made it*.
- **Trade secrets** protect the Coca-Cola formula and the recipe for your favorite fried-chicken chain's seasoning, not by registration but by secrecy itself: locked vaults, need-to-know access, and nondisclosure agreements.
- **Patents** protect the mechanisms inside your phone: thousands of them per device, which is why phone makers sue each other so often.

### Patents vs. Trade Secrets: A Strategic Tradeoff

Why does Coca-Cola keep its formula secret instead of patenting it? Because the two protections make opposite bargains. A **patent** requires you to *publish* your invention in full detail; in exchange, you get a legal monopoly for 20 years, after which anyone may use it. A **trade secret** requires disclosure to no one and can last forever, but the moment someone independently invents it, reverse-engineers it, or leaks it, protection evaporates. So the choice is strategic: patent what competitors could figure out anyway (drug formulas are patented because chemists can analyze a pill), and keep secret what they cannot (a soft-drink recipe has resisted reverse engineering for over a century). Google's search-ranking algorithms are trade secrets; a patent would have expired decades ago.

### Trademark's Strange Failure Mode: Genericide

A trademark lasts indefinitely, but only while it identifies a *particular producer*. If the public starts using the brand name as the ordinary word for the product itself, the mark can be canceled: a fate called **genericide**. It has happened to *aspirin*, *escalator*, *thermos*, and *trampoline*, all once exclusive brand names. This is why companies send stern letters to journalists who write "googling" or "photoshopped": lawyers policing everyday speech in a fight against their own brand's popularity.

## Copyright Basics

### What Copyright Protects

Copyright automatically protects original creative works:
- Literary works (books, articles, code)
- Musical compositions and recordings
- Visual art (photographs, paintings)
- Movies and videos
- Architectural works

"Automatically" is the key word: the moment you fix an original work in tangible form (write the essay, snap the photo, record the riff), copyright exists. No registration, no fee, no © symbol required. You have been accumulating copyrights your whole life.

### What Copyright DOESN'T Protect

- Ideas (only the expression of ideas)
- Facts
- Titles, names, short phrases
- Works in the public domain
- **Works generated entirely by AI** (more on this below!)

The idea/expression line deserves a beat: copyright protects *how you said it*, never *what you said*. Anyone may write a novel about a boy wizard at a magic school; no one may copy J.K. Rowling's sentences or her particular characters. Facts are free to all, which is why journalists can report each other's scoops and databases of raw facts get thin protection.

### The Public Domain

The **public domain** is the pool of creative work that belongs to everyone, free to copy, adapt, remix, and sell. Works arrive there by several routes: copyright *expires* (in the US, works published before a rolling cutoff enter the public domain each January 1, a date fans of old cartoons now celebrate as Public Domain Day; the earliest Mickey Mouse cartoon, *Steamboat Willie*, entered in 2024); the work was *never eligible* (facts, US federal government works, and, as you'll see below, purely AI-generated output); or the creator *dedicates* it voluntarily. Every Disney fairy-tale blockbuster is built on public-domain source material, which gives the company's long history of lobbying to extend copyright terms a certain irony worth discussing.

## Fair Use

**Fair Use** allows limited use of copyrighted material without permission for purposes like:

- **Criticism and Commentary**
- **News Reporting**
- **Teaching and Education**
- **Research**
- **Parody**

### The Four Factors

Courts consider:
1. **Purpose**: Is it commercial or educational? Is the use *transformative*?
2. **Nature**: Is the original work creative or factual?
3. **Amount**: How much did you use?
4. **Effect**: Does it harm the market for the original?

Each factor is a sliding scale, not a checkbox, and courts weigh all four together. A mini-example for each:

1. **Purpose and character.** A film critic quoting dialogue to review a movie leans fair (commentary, noncommercial-ish, adds new meaning). A t-shirt company printing the same dialogue to sell shirts leans unfair (commercial, adds nothing). Parody gets special protection: 2 Live Crew's raunchy remake of "Oh, Pretty Woman" was fair use precisely because parody *must* borrow from its target.
2. **Nature of the original.** Quoting a factual news report leans fair; copying from an unpublished novel leans unfair, because creative and unpublished works get stronger protection than factual ones. Facts want to circulate; art gets more shelter.
3. **Amount and substantiality.** Quoting two sentences of a book leans fair; posting a chapter does not. But quality matters as much as quantity: a magazine that quoted only about 300 words from President Ford's memoir still lost, because those words (the Nixon pardon explanation) were "the heart of the work."
4. **Market effect.** Recording a TV show to watch later was fair use (no market harm, said the Supreme Court in the Betamax case); posting the same show to YouTube substitutes for the original and harms its market. Ask: does the use compete with, or replace, the original or its licensing market?

### Transformative Use, and What *Warhol* Changed

Running through factor one is the idea of **transformative use**: does the new work add new expression, meaning, or purpose rather than merely repackaging the original? For years, many assumed that making something look or feel different was enough. The Supreme Court corrected that assumption in *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith* (2023). Photographer Lynn Goldsmith had taken a portrait of Prince; Warhol silkscreened it into his instantly recognizable pop-art style; decades later the Warhol Foundation licensed the resulting *Orange Prince* image to a magazine for a Prince cover story. The Court held, 7 to 2, that the first factor turns on the purpose of the *use*, not on how visually different the new work is. Licensing an image of Prince to illustrate a magazine article about Prince was exactly the commercial purpose Goldsmith's photo served, so factor one favored Goldsmith. Warhol's artistic transformation did not settle the question; the competing *use* did. The practical lesson for you: "I changed it a lot" is not a fair-use argument by itself. Ask instead whether your use serves a different purpose from the original or substitutes for it in the same market.

:::{warning} Fair Use is Not Clear-Cut
Fair use is determined case-by-case. When in doubt, get permission or use openly licensed content. And note what fair use is *not*: it is a defense you argue after being sued, not a license you hold in advance. Even musicians with expert lawyers guess wrong in both directions; Ed Sheeran spent years in court before a jury found in 2023 that "Thinking Out Loud" did not infringe Marvin Gaye's "Let's Get It On."
:::

## AI and Copyright: The Courts Have Started Answering

When this course was first taught, every AI copyright question was "pending." That is no longer true, because the last two years produced landmark rulings:

| Question | What's Been Decided |
|----------|---------------------|
| **Can AI-generated work be copyrighted?** | **No; this is settled law.** In *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, the D.C. Circuit affirmed (March 2025) that copyright requires a *human* author; the Supreme Court declined to hear the appeal in 2026 ([opinion](https://media.cadc.uscourts.gov/opinions/docs/2025/03/23-5233.pdf)). The [US Copyright Office's AI reports](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/) add nuance: works made *with* AI assistance can be protected to the extent of the human contribution, but a text prompt alone isn't enough. |
| **Can AI train on copyrighted books?** | **It depends how you got them.** In *Bartz v. Anthropic* (June 2025), a federal judge ruled that training on *lawfully purchased* books is transformative fair use, but that keeping a library of *pirated* copies is not. Anthropic then settled the piracy claims for **$1.5 billion**, the largest copyright settlement in US history ([analysis](https://legalblogs.wolterskluwer.com/copyright-blog/the-bartz-v-anthropic-settlement-understanding-americas-largest-copyright-settlement/)). *Kadrey v. Meta* (June 2025) also went Meta's way, but the judge pointedly left the door open to future "market dilution" arguments. |
| **Is it over?** | **No.** *The New York Times v. OpenAI*, the highest-profile case and the one with the strongest market-harm argument, is still being litigated as of mid-2026. The next few years will keep reshaping this table. |

It is worth appreciating how fast this moved. When authors including Michael Chabon first sued OpenAI in September 2023, scholars like Berkeley's Pamela Samuelson framed the core claim as untested: is ingesting copyrighted works as training data itself an infringing reproduction? Barely two years later, courts had answered "the ingestion can be fair use, the piracy cannot," and a $1.5 billion settlement had put a price on the difference. The four factors you just learned are precisely the tool the judges used; the *Warhol* purpose-of-the-use lens shaped how they asked whether training "supersedes" books or does something genuinely different with them.

:::{tip} Apply the Four Factors Yourself
Take the *Bartz* facts: an AI company buys millions of books, scans them, and trains a model that doesn't reproduce the books but learned from all of them. Walk through purpose, nature, amount, and market effect. Would *you* have called it fair use? Now change one fact (the books were pirated) and see which factor flips. This is exactly how the court reasoned.
:::

### 📌 Case Study: *Hachette v. Internet Archive*

The Internet Archive scanned physical books it owned and lent the scans one-at-a-time, like a library: "controlled digital lending." Publishers sued. The Second Circuit ruled in September 2024 that this was **not** fair use, and the Archive [ended its appeal](https://blog.archive.org/2024/12/04/end-of-hachette-v-internet-archive/). For an information school, this case cuts close to home: it draws the current legal line between *owning* a book and *lending access* to it digitally. Do you think the line is in the right place?

### Personality, Voice, and Deepfakes

Two new laws extend "ownership" to your face and voice:
- **Tennessee's ELVIS Act (2024)**: the first state law protecting musicians' *voices* from unauthorized AI cloning
- **The TAKE IT DOWN Act (2025)**: federal criminal penalties for nonconsensual intimate images, explicitly including AI deepfakes ([Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/146))

## Net Neutrality: A Case Study in Regulatory Whiplash

**Net Neutrality** is the principle that internet service providers (ISPs) should treat all internet traffic equally, without discriminating based on source, destination, or content.

### What Would ISPs Actually Do? The Vocabulary

The debate is easier to follow once the abstract principle is translated into the four specific behaviors the rules addressed:

- **Blocking**: the ISP refuses to carry certain traffic at all, such as a competitor's video app or a disfavored website. Under neutrality rules, if content is legal, the ISP must carry it.
- **Throttling**: the ISP deliberately slows particular traffic. The most vivid US example was not even about content: in 2018, Verizon throttled the data connection of the Santa Clara County Fire Department *while it was fighting the Mendocino Complex wildfire*, then told the department the fix was upgrading to a pricier plan. The incident (which Verizon called a customer-service error) became Exhibit A for why "just trust the ISPs" reassures few people.
- **Paid prioritization**: the ISP sells "fast lanes," delivering the traffic of companies that pay extra ahead of everyone else's. Netflix can afford the toll; the next startup competing with Netflix cannot. Critics argue this quietly reshapes who can compete on the internet.
- **Zero-rating**: certain services do not count against your data cap. It feels like a gift (free streaming!), but when the ISP zero-rates *its own* streaming service and not rivals', the "gift" steers your choices. Regulators worldwide have split on whether this counts as discrimination.

The point of the vocabulary: with no federal rules in force (see the timeline below), whether any of these practices is forbidden currently depends mostly on which state you live in.

### The Rise and Fall (and Rise and Fall)

| Year | What Happened |
|------|---------------|
| 2015 | FCC adopts the Open Internet Order: broadband reclassified under "Title II," net neutrality rules in force |
| 2017 | New FCC majority **repeals** the rules |
| April 2024 | FCC **restores** Title II net neutrality rules |
| January 2025 | The Sixth Circuit **strikes the rules down** (*Ohio Telecom Ass'n v. FCC*), ruling the FCC lacked authority to reclassify broadband; the court applied the Supreme Court's 2024 *Loper Bright* decision, which ended judicial deference to agencies ([opinion](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca6/24-3449/24-3449-2025-01-02.html)) |
| Today | **No federal net neutrality rules.** The FCC did not appeal. The action has moved to **state laws**, where California's SB 822 (upheld in court) leads a patchwork of state protections, and to the question of whether Congress will ever settle it |

A note on the "Title II" jargon in that timeline: reclassifying broadband under Title II of the Communications Act meant treating ISPs as **common carriers**, the same legal category as telephone companies, which must serve everyone on equal terms. That single classification question is what the entire decade-long fight was legally *about*, and it is what the Sixth Circuit ultimately said the FCC could not decide on its own.

### Why Teach a Dead Rule?

Because the *story* is the lesson. In ten years, net neutrality went in-force → repealed → restored → struck down, without the underlying technology or arguments changing much. What changed was politics and, crucially, **administrative law**: courts now defer far less to agencies like the FCC. For anyone working in tech policy, this whiplash, along with the shift of the battleground from Washington to state capitals, is the reality of how technology law gets made.

There is also a deeper stake than streaming speeds, one former FCC chair Tom Wheeler urged people not to lose sight of: the framing of "blocking and throttling" understates the question of whether a pathway so many Americans rely on should carry any public-interest obligations at all. Net neutrality connects directly to the **digital divide** (Module 2): if internet access shapes education, employment, and even health outcomes (public-health agencies now describe broadband as a "super determinant" of health, since telehealth, job applications, and schoolwork all run through it), then who controls the terms of access is a social question, not merely a commercial one. Equal treatment of traffic keeps costs predictable for low-income users, keeps the playing field level for small content creators, and keeps ISPs out of the business of deciding what information their subscribers encounter.

### The Debate (Still Worth Having)

| Pro-Net Neutrality | Against Net Neutrality |
|-------------------|----------------------|
| Protects free speech and innovation | Allows market-based solutions |
| Prevents ISPs from favoring their own content | Enables investment in infrastructure |
| Keeps internet open and fair | Different services have different needs |
| Supports small businesses and startups | Regulation stifles innovation |

The right-hand column deserves a fair hearing too: ISPs argue that some traffic genuinely is more time-sensitive (a video call suffers from delay; an email does not), that fast lanes could fund network buildout, and that twenty years of a mostly unregulated internet produced explosive innovation. The question to sit with: which side's risks worry you more, and who should bear the burden of proof?

## Open Licensing Alternatives

Not all content is traditionally copyrighted:

| License Type | What It Means |
|--------------|---------------|
| **Public Domain** | No copyright restrictions; free to use |
| **Creative Commons** | Author-chosen permissions (attribution, non-commercial, etc.) |
| **Open Source** | Software with freely available source code |
| **Copyleft** | Derivative works must use the same license |
