## What is Intellectual Property?

**Intellectual Property (IP)** refers to creations of the mind that are legally protected.

### Types of IP Protection

| Type | Protects | Duration | Example |
|------|----------|----------|---------|
| **Copyright** | Creative works | Life + 70 years | Books, music, movies |
| **Patents** | Inventions | 20 years (from filing) | New technology, processes |
| **Trademarks** | Brand identifiers | Indefinite (if renewed) | Logos, brand names |
| **Trade Secrets** | Confidential business info | As long as kept secret | Formulas, processes |

### IP in Your Daily Life

The table looks abstract until you realize you brush against all four types before lunch:

- **Copyright** governs the meme you reposted this morning (someone owns that screenshot or photo), the song sample in a track you streamed (recording artists license samples or get sued; the hip-hop sampling wars of the 1990s built a whole licensing industry), the fan fiction you read (technically a derivative work of the original author's characters, usually tolerated but rarely *licensed*), and the code you copy from a tutorial (open-source licenses are copyright licenses).
- **Trademarks** are why the swoosh on your shoes, the golden arches you passed, and even a specific shade of robin's-egg blue on a jewelry box are legally protected. A trademark does not protect the *thing*; it protects consumers from confusion about *who made it*.
- **Trade secrets** protect the Coca-Cola formula and the recipe for your favorite fried-chicken chain's seasoning, not by registration but by secrecy itself: locked vaults, need-to-know access, and nondisclosure agreements.
- **Patents** protect the mechanisms inside your phone: thousands of them per device, which is why phone makers sue each other so often.

### Patents vs. Trade Secrets: A Strategic Tradeoff

Why does Coca-Cola keep its formula secret instead of patenting it? Because the two protections make opposite bargains. A **patent** requires you to *publish* your invention in full detail; in exchange, you get a legal monopoly for 20 years, after which anyone may use it. A **trade secret** requires disclosure to no one and can last forever, but the moment someone independently invents it, reverse-engineers it, or leaks it, protection evaporates. So the choice is strategic: patent what competitors could figure out anyway (drug formulas are patented because chemists can analyze a pill), and keep secret what they cannot (a soft-drink recipe has resisted reverse engineering for over a century). Google's search-ranking algorithms are trade secrets; a patent would have expired decades ago.

### Trademark's Strange Failure Mode: Genericide

A trademark lasts indefinitely, but only while it identifies a *particular producer*. If the public starts using the brand name as the ordinary word for the product itself, the mark can be canceled: a fate called **genericide**. It has happened to *aspirin*, *escalator*, *thermos*, and *trampoline*, all once exclusive brand names. This is why companies send stern letters to journalists who write "googling" or "photoshopped": lawyers policing everyday speech in a fight against their own brand's popularity.
