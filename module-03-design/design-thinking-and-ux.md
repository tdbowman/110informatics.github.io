## Design Thinking

**Design thinking** is a non-linear, iterative process that teams use to understand users, challenge assumptions, redefine problems, and create innovative solutions to prototype and test. It is most useful for tackling ill-defined or unknown problems, the kind where you're not even sure you're solving the right problem yet. It involves five phases:

1. **Empathize**: Understand users and their needs. This means observing and talking with real people, not imagining them from a conference room. What are their gaps (remember [Dervin](understanding-users.md))? What mental models do they hold?
2. **Define**: Clearly articulate the problem. Teams often discover during empathy work that the real problem differs from the one they were assigned. "Students don't use the library website" might really be "students can't tell which of five search boxes to use."
3. **Ideate**: Brainstorm potential solutions, widely and without early judgment. Quantity first, filtering later.
4. **Prototype**: Build cheap, fast representations of solutions. A paper sketch, a clickable mockup, a role-played script. The point is to make ideas concrete enough to test.
5. **Test**: Try solutions with real users, watch what happens, and loop back. Testing almost always sends you back to an earlier phase, and that's the process working, not failing.

The phases are numbered, but the process is deliberately not a straight line. Real projects bounce between phases as each round of testing teaches the team something new about the problem.

## Human-Centered Design vs. Design Thinking

You'll also hear the term **human-centered design (HCD)**: a methodology that places the user at the heart of the design process. HCD seeks to deeply understand users' needs, behaviors, and experiences, and it emphasizes empathy, extensive user research, and iterative testing to ensure the final product genuinely benefits the people who use it.

So what's the difference? Think of it this way: human-centered design is the *philosophy* (whatever we build, the human comes first), while design thinking is a *process* you can follow to act on that philosophy (the five phases above). You can practice human-centered design using many processes, and you could technically run the five design-thinking phases while ignoring users, though at that point you'd just be making expensive guesses. In practice the terms overlap heavily, and both stand in contrast to technology-centered design, where a team builds what's technically interesting and hopes users adapt.

## HCI, UCD, UI, & UX

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **HCI** | Human-Computer Interaction: academic study of how people interact with technology |
| **UCD** | User-Centered Design: designing with the user's needs as primary focus |
| **UI** | User Interface: the visual elements users interact with |
| **UX** | User Experience: the overall experience of using a product |

These four terms form a family with a rough division of labor. HCI is the oldest: a multidisciplinary research field focused on the design of computer technology and the interaction between humans and computers, drawing on psychology, computer science, and design. HCI practitioners tend to be academically focused, running studies and building empirical understandings of users. UX designers, by contrast, are almost invariably industry-focused, building actual products like apps and websites. In many ways, HCI was the forerunner to UX design.

The historical shift matters. Early HCI focused on the achievement of behavioral goals in work settings: could a trained operator complete tasks quickly and without errors? UX broadens that lens to include non-instrumental aspects of interaction: Is it pleasurable? Is it beautiful? Does it make you feel competent, or foolish? A system can score perfectly on task completion and still be an experience nobody wants to repeat.

UI is the narrowest term of the four: the actual screens, buttons, typography, and layouts. A gorgeous UI inside a confusing UX is like a beautifully decorated room in a building with no clear exits.

## The Experiential Perspective

One of this week's big questions deserves a closer look: can we design an experience, or only design *for* an experience?

The experiential perspective in UX says experiences have two defining properties. **Situatedness** means every experience happens in a context: the same app feels different on a crowded bus than at your desk. **Temporality** means experiences unfold over time: they have a beginning and an end, and your memory of them keeps changing after they're over.

Researchers also distinguish "an experience" from "experience" in general. *An experience* is something you can name and narrate: your first video call with a faraway relative, the night your team's group chat saved a project. It has a beginning and an end, and it can inspire behavioral and emotional change. It is complex, unique, and hard to repeat. *Experience*, by contrast, is the constant stream of self-talk running while you interact with products: "where's the button... okay... wait, did that save?"

The honest answer to the big question is that designers cannot manufacture experiences directly, because experience emerges from the meeting of a product, a person, and a moment. What designers can do is set the stage: shape the conditions, remove the frustrations, and create openings for good experiences to happen. That humility, designing *for* experience rather than claiming to design experience itself, is a hallmark of mature UX thinking.
